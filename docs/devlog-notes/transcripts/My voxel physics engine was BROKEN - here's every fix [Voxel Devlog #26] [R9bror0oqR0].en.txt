So, now it's time to talk about how it all works, starting with the collision detector. As a recap, the collision detector is what the engine uses to figure out which objects are touching and keep them apart. In a typical game engine, you just have a few different kinds of colliders. You can have spheres and boxes and capsules. And there's basically a mathematical formula that's implemented to figure out if they overlap. But for voxel data, like I said, we want to support collisions between any two arbitrary objects. And so, the collision detector needs to operate on the voxel data itself. The way this is done is by first labeling every voxel as either a corner, edge, face, or interior. Because collisions can only occur between two edge voxels or a corner voxel and another corner, face, or edge. So, this narrows down the search space of which voxels you have to check. &gt;&gt; And then, during collision detection, you simply iterate over all of those voxels in some way, the corners and the edges in particular, transform them into the coordinate system of the other object being checked, and determine whether there are any voxels nearby. If the voxels are overlapping, you determine a penetration depth and a normal direction. And that normal direction is really important because it tells the engine which direction the objects need to be pushed to keep them apart. That last step is what was kind of janky in my previous physics implementation. To determine whether two nearby voxels were overlapping, I would literally translate the center of one voxel into the other coordinate system, then look at its bounding box in the new coordinate system, and compare that to the bounding box of the other voxel. But this algorithm isn't really invariant under rotation. Like, if we were to rotate the um initiator object here just a little bit, the axis-aligned bounding box doesn't change properly &gt;&gt; in the target coordinate system. And so, this was leading to inaccurate normals and lots of jitter. The way Teardown handles this is by treating the voxels as spheres during this final step of collision detection. So, it'll translate one voxel into the other coordinate system, but then perform a sphere versus sphere test. Since spheres are invariant under rotation, this sort of avoids the normal issue. And it does a little bit of fancy stuff where like if one of the voxels is a face, it will clamp the normal to be pointing outward. Next is the solver. The solver is the heart of any rigid body simulation. It's responsible for applying forces to objects like gravity and maintaining constraints to keep objects apart. In my previous engine, I used what would best be described as a split impulses solver [music] and the main loop looked something like this. First, external forces would be applied to bodies and then velocities and positions would be integrated, so all bodies would be moved slightly. Then collision detection would run to find points of overlap. Then the velocities of objects would be adjusted to prevent those points of overlap from sinking deeper into one another. And then finally after that, the positions of objects would be adjusted in order to separate them. This approach already has a problem, which is that even an object sitting stationary on the ground is going to move ever so slightly each frame. So, imagine a box sitting still. Every frame gravity is applied to it, which causes it to move slightly downward. Then contact points are generated for the corners and the velocities of those points are zeroed out and then the box is sort of pushed upward as well a little bit. Due to the limited precision of floating point numbers, the box getting pushed up and down and up and down can make it jitter. We can do much better by integrating position only after the velocity constraints have already been solved. So, in the example of the box, contact points would be generated and the box's velocity would be zeroed in order to prevent those contacts from going any deeper. Then, if we updated the boxes position, its transform wouldn't change because its velocity is already zero. Even with this insight, there are multiple ways you can structure your simulation loop and I tried out a couple of different possibilities including PGS and NGS. But the approach I found that seemed to work best was one called TGS or temporal Gauss-Seidel [music] and here's what it looks like in my engine. We start out by detecting collisions between body pairs. Then, we integrate forces updating the velocity of each object. After that, velocity constraints are solved using the contact points that were generated earlier and so this ensures that objects aren't moving in a way that would cause them to interpenetrate more. After that, we update object positions based upon their new velocities. And this inner loop of adding forces, solving velocities, and then updating position this is run multiple times and so it's almost like the physics engine is taking multiple steps in one, but it's not rerunning collision detection. It's reusing the existing contacts. And so this leads to an algorithm that converges much faster. The other thing that improved convergence was to use warm starting. When the engine is solving the aforementioned velocity constraints, what it's truly doing is finding a normal force to oppose gravity or other forces in the scene. In the simplest implementation, [music] you would initialize this force to zero and then iteratively try to figure out what the correct [music] value should be.
